**Pigeons**

There are many pigeons in the city where I live.

They wander around stations, churches, and the entrances to cafés, heads bowed, moving one measured step at a time, as though searching for something or as though they have simply grown accustomed to living this way.

Most people in the city are fond of animals.

On fine days, people often break bread into pieces and scatter them beside the benches. Children run among the pigeons while adults stand nearby taking photographs. In the middle of the square, however, stands a single sign. It says that feeding pigeons makes them lose the ability to forage and also compromises the cleanliness of the city.

I think there is good sense in that.

When there are too many pigeons, statues and eaves are easily fouled. Besides, they have wings, after all. Since they can fly, it seems only natural that they should find their own food.

An old woman often sits beside the sign.

Every day she brings a small bag of oats and, when the inspector is away, quietly scatters them at her feet. The pigeons seem to know her; the moment she sits down, the space around her gradually comes alive.

She told me that she did not feed every pigeon.

“I only feed the few that were here originally,” she said.

She can tell them apart. She knows which one has an injured leg, which one has the brighter feathers on its neck, and which one arrives first each morning.

As for those that flew in later, she said, you could tell them at a glance.

That day, a dark-grey pigeon forced its way into the flock, knocking aside a scrawny white pigeon before lowering its head to peck at the oats. The old woman immediately put out the toe of her shoe and drove it away.

The pigeon retreated a few steps.

After a while, it came back.

The old woman drove it away again. Still it did not fly. It merely withdrew a little farther and stood there watching.

“These newcomers are all so aggressive,” the old woman said. “They don’t know the rules.”

I looked at the pigeon.

Its feathers were indeed a little more dishevelled, its colouring darker. Standing amid a flock of white pigeons, it was conspicuous.

Before long, the inspector arrived.

The old woman immediately put away her bag of oats and said that she was only sitting there to rest. The inspector did not reprimand her, offering only a courteous reminder that feeding pigeons at will affected the environment and would draw more and more of them to this place.

“Of course,” the old woman said. “Too many of them really are a problem.”

After the inspector left, she waited a while, then reached into the bag again.

The pigeons gathered around her once more.

The dark-grey pigeon was still standing at a distance. It made no further attempt to come near, only lowering its head now and then to peck at something invisible between the stones.

Once several of the white pigeons had eaten their fill, they began to chase another, smaller white pigeon. Taking turns, they pecked at its back and drove it from one side of the square to the other.

The old woman watched for a while, then laughed.

“That’s just how pigeons are,” she said.

I laughed too.

Later, I went nearby and bought some bread.

I had barely sat down on a bench when two pigeons slowly approached. One was white. The other was the dark-grey one.

I was about to break off a little bread when I suddenly remembered the sign in the middle of the square.

Feeding makes pigeons lose their ability to survive. It also compromises the cleanliness of the city.

So I paid them no attention.

After a while, the dark-grey pigeon was the first to leave.

The white pigeon remained where it was, looking up at me.

I glanced around, broke off a small piece of bread, and tossed it down in front of the bird.

That did not count as feeding pigeons, of course.

I simply recognised this one.
